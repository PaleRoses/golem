# Solver-assisted surface formation for skeleton-based procedural creatures

## Scope and reading rule

This is a literature sweep about **formation of musculature, flesh, and skin from a skeleton**, not a survey of mesh skinning in general and not a request to import a human scan or an interactive sculpting workflow. The target is a deterministic kernel in which authored intent is a finite, symbolic input; a solver or constructive operator derives a surface; and acceptance returns a typed verdict rather than silently emitting a plausible-looking failure product.

The present GOLEM seam is unusually explicit. `golem/kernel/body/myology.py` resolves authored muscle declarations into `gencyl` parts. `golem/kernel/engine/types.py` defines the graph contract and the `blend`, `chamfer`, and `crease` composition vocabulary. `golem/kernel/engine/algebra.py` owns the primitive fields and the polynomial `smin`. `golem/kernel/engine/compile.py` samples those fields in authored order, glues the union, subtracts carves, and lowers the result to an iso-surface. The current symptom is therefore not mysterious: the semantic result is an ordered union of authored capsule/loft-like fields, and a global smooth-min is being asked to impersonate anatomy.

The papers below are ranked by what they can contribute to that seam. “What it actually does” reports the published method, including its dependencies on meshes, user interaction, data, or physical simulation. “Adaptation to the kernel” is deliberately stricter: it says what can be made deterministic and symbolic, what must remain a derived view, and what obstruction would have to be reported instead of hidden.

## 1. Implicit-surface composition operators

### 1.1 Controllable Binary CSG Operators for “Soft Objects”

**Citation.** Loïc Barthe, Brian Wyvill, and Erwin de Groot. “Controllable Binary CSG Operators for ‘Soft Objects’.” *International Journal of Shape Modeling*, 10(2), 135–154, 2004. [DOI: 10.1142/S021865430400064X](https://doi.org/10.1142/S021865430400064X).

**What it actually does.** This paper treats bounded implicit fields as solids and asks for binary union, intersection, and difference operators that can retain either a smooth transition or a sharp transition in a controlled location. Its operator is not the ordinary sum of potentials: it reasons about the field values and uses an opening-angle parameter to select where a clean set-like union gives way to a controlled blend. The result is a bounded, field-based CSG operation that can keep the outside behavior of the operands while shaping the transition near their contact. It is the “Barthe blend” later discussed by Gourmel et al.; it is not a general-purpose physics solve and it does not infer anatomy.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is a direct replacement candidate for the present `smin` seam when two authored tissues are intended to meet but not inflate each other globally. The public input should be a closed `CompositionOperator` plus a bounded transition policy, not a raw blend radius whose meaning changes with field scale. The operator can be pure and pointwise, preserve exact hard union outside its active region, and expose a deterministic active-region witness. The main obstruction is field normalization: the operator assumes comparable field semantics, while current primitives are only distance-like approximations. A solver-assisted adoption must therefore either normalize each operand with a certified gradient/Lipschitz bound or reject `UncalibratedFieldComposition`; pretending that a raw `smin` radius is anatomically meaningful would merely give the old ghost a new name.

### 1.2 Bounded Blending Operations

**Citation.** Galina Pasko, Alexander A. Pasko, M. Ikeda, and Tosiyasu L. Kunii. “Bounded Blending Operations.” In *Proceedings of the International Conference on Shape Modeling and Applications (SMI 2002)*, 95–103, IEEE Computer Society, 2002. [DOI: 10.1109/SMI.2002.1003533](https://doi.org/10.1109/SMI.2002.1003533).

**What it actually does.** The authors formulate localized set-theoretic blending with R-functions and displacement functions. A blend can be bounded by control points placed on the two solids or by a third implicit bounding solid; the construction supports localized union, intersection, and difference, including partial-edge and multiple blends. The third object is semantic, not decorative: it specifies the region in which the set operation is allowed to deviate from the clean result. The construction tree remains a function representation, so the output is still evaluable as a real field rather than a one-off mesh patch.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This gives a typed ternary composition node: `BoundedBlend(left, right, region, policy)`. For a skeleton creature, the region can be derived from the local overlap of two muscle envelopes or from a junction cell around a bone fork, rather than authored as an arbitrary sculpt. That local region is exactly the sort of interface object the kernel currently lacks: the two local flesh sections can remain individually authoritative, while the junction owns only the compatibility correction. A deterministic implementation should construct the region from certified intersection/clearance bounds and reject disconnected, ambiguous, or non-transverse overlap instead of selecting a random branch. The benefit is architectural: blend influence becomes an explicit local section with a gluing obligation, not a global side effect of traversal order.

### 1.3 Bounded Blending for Function-Based Shape Modeling

**Citation.** Galina I. Pasko, Alexander A. Pasko, and Tosiyasu L. Kunii. “Bounded Blending for Function-Based Shape Modeling.” *IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications*, 25(2), 36–45, 2005. [DOI: 10.1109/MCG.2005.37](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCG.2005.37).

**What it actually does.** This is the journal treatment of the bounded-blend construction. It formalizes R-function and displacement-function operators whose area of influence is localized by surface control points or an additional bounding solid, and demonstrates partial-edge, multiple, and recursive blends. The important result is not visual softness; it is an analytic operation whose support can be stated and whose result remains closed under further function composition. This is a useful response to the classic failure mode in which a smooth implicit union changes an entire small primitive because a much larger primitive contributes field value far away.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The paper suggests a cheap, high-leverage law for junctions: every smooth transition must carry a finite support witness, and outside that witness the result must equal a clean union or another declared operation. In GOLEM terms, the witness belongs in the solved morphology receipt and can be checked at the same time as topology and clearance. It is adaptable without human scans or an interactive editor, provided the support solid is derived from bone-local anchors, radii, and the finite junction graph. It is not enough to add a `bounded=True` flag: the implementation must prove containment of the modified zero set and report a typed obstruction when support geometry misses the actual overlap.

### 1.4 Implicit Blending Revisited

**Citation.** Adrien Bernhardt, Loïc Barthe, Marie-Paule Cani, and Brian Wyvill. “Implicit Blending Revisited.” *Computer Graphics Forum*, 29(2), 367–375, 2010. [DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-8659.2009.01606.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2009.01606.x).

**What it actually does.** Bernhardt et al. identify the central controllability defect of functionally based implicit models: ordinary blends begin before objects overlap, and the effect depends on field slopes. Their method automatically defines blending regions around intersection curves. Outside those regions it uses a smooth clean-union operator; inside them it uses a smooth blend, with parameters that can be tuned to prevent a small object from being swallowed by a larger one and to make contact blending progressive during animation. The paper is a composition method, not an anatomical reconstruction method and not a variational tissue solver.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the best conceptual successor to the current global smooth-min for authored muscle interfaces. It preserves the local-to-global distinction: each pair or junction has an overlap section, the section has a compatible local blend, and the whole creature is obtained by gluing those sections. The deterministic version should derive intersection curves or bounded overlap cells from the two fields, impose a stable ordering over junctions, and carry residuals for clean-union agreement and contact containment. It also gives a principled answer to “blending at a distance”: a non-overlapping pair remains a disjoint pair unless the author explicitly declares a bridge. If the fields have no reliable common iso-value or the intersection has multiple incompatible components, the solver should return a typed interface obstruction rather than collapsing them with a larger radius.

### 1.5 A Gradient-Based Implicit Blend

**Citation.** Olivier Gourmel, Loïc Barthe, Marie-Paule Cani, Brian Wyvill, Adrien Bernhardt, Mathias Paulin, and Herbert Grasberger. “A Gradient-Based Implicit Blend.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 32(2), Article 12, 12 pages, 2013. [DOI: 10.1145/2451236.2451238](https://doi.org/10.1145/2451236.2451238).

**What it actually does.** Gourmel et al. introduce binary composition operators whose decisions depend on field gradients rather than only field values. The method targets four failures of constructive implicit modeling: bulges when shapes merge, unwanted blending at a distance, topology that no longer matches the intended union, and sharp details that get rounded or blown up by the blend. It defines an opening between union and blend behavior using local gradient information, allowing sharp features to pass through without becoming part of a broad smooth transition. The result is still an analytic field composition; it does not solve a muscle volume, infer fiber directions, or simulate skin.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the strongest first adoption for the existing primitive field algebra. The current `smin` consumes `(a, b, k)` and has no knowledge of gradient scale, local feature size, or whether the operands overlap. A gradient-aware operator can keep the existing pure sampling architecture while changing the law at `compose_union`: normalize local gradients, derive a contact-local blend width, and retain a hard or clean union where compatibility says the operands should not merge. A typed verdict should record gradient bounds, the chosen branch, and topology checks; if gradients vanish, reverse, or are too poorly resolved for the promised guarantee, return a `GradientCalibrationObstruction`. This is not a full solver-assisted surface formation system, but it removes the most expensive visual lie at the cheapest semantic boundary.

### 1.6 Sketch-Based Implicit Blending

**Citation.** Baptiste Angles, Marco Tarini, Brian Wyvill, Loïc Barthe, and Andrea Tagliasacchi. “Sketch-Based Implicit Blending.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 36(6), Article 181, 13 pages, 2017. [DOI: 10.1145/3130800.3130825](https://doi.org/10.1145/3130800.3130825).

**What it actually does.** This later work treats the problem in reverse: rather than choosing a field operator and accepting whatever transition it creates, it lets a user specify a desired transition shape and solves for an implicit composition that realizes it. The paper introduces a general template for implicit operators and handles the inverse problem of recovering a function from a shape, in both 2D and 3D. It is explicitly an interactive design system, so its authoring mechanism is not suitable for the kernel as-is; its useful contribution is the parameterization of a family of operators by a target interface condition.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** Replace the sketch with symbolic interface intent: desired neck width, tangent continuity, ridge height, or preserved feature order at a named muscle junction. The operator family can then be selected by a finite solver over a closed policy vocabulary, with the target condition becoming a residual and the output carrying the chosen coefficients as derived evidence. This preserves the paper's inverse-design insight without importing a stroke editor or data-driven fitting. The boundary must be strict: a target condition that has no solution in the chosen operator family is `BlendIntentUnsatisfied`, not an invitation to widen the blend until something vaguely organic appears.

### 1.7 Feature Based Volumes for Implicit Intersections

**Citation.** Oleg Fryazinov, Pierre-Alain Fayolle, Turlif Vilbrandt, Galina Pasko, and Alexander Pasko. “Feature Based Volumes for Implicit Intersections.” *Computers & Graphics*, 35(3), 524–531, 2011. [DOI: 10.1016/j.cag.2011.03.029](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cag.2011.03.029).

**What it actually does.** The paper automatically constructs a volume around all components of the intersection of two implicit surfaces. It uses constructive operations, function normalization, and offsetting; in the curve case, an approximated intersection skeleton can generate the feature volume through a distance or convolution construction. The resulting feature-based volume can bound a blend, render intersection features, or host surface-based structures. Unlike a manually placed bounding solid, it is derived from the actual two operands and can handle intersection components of different dimensions.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is a useful solver subproblem for Bernhardt/Pasko-style composition. Given two bone-local fields, derive a finite junction region and use it as the only place where a tissue-interface operator may alter the clean union. The derivation can be deterministic if intersection component ordering, tolerances, and approximation budgets are sealed. The kernel must retain the distinction between an exact intersection proof and a sampled candidate: a candidate FBV with unresolved components is an obstruction, not a license to blend the entire pair. This method is particularly attractive for authored intent because the author names the two tissues and their intended relationship; the region is a derived view of their compatibility.

## 2. Skeleton-integral surfaces, canal surfaces, and sweep fields

### 2.1 Convolution Surfaces

**Citation.** Jules Bloomenthal and Ken Shoemake. “Convolution Surfaces.” In *Proceedings of the 18th Annual Conference on Computer Graphics and Interactive Techniques (SIGGRAPH ’91)*, *Computer Graphics*, 25(4), 251–256, ACM, 1991. [DOI: 10.1145/127719.122757](https://doi.org/10.1145/127719.122757).

**What it actually does.** Bloomenthal and Shoemake extend point-potential or blobby modeling by convolving a spatial kernel with skeletons made from curves and polygons. The field is an integral over the skeleton, so splitting a skeleton into pieces does not create the same seam behavior as separately summing independent primitive fields. The resulting iso-surface supports seamless joins, fluid topology changes, and an efficient implementation, and was demonstrated on articulated forms including a human arm. The cost is that the chosen kernel and iso-value determine thickness indirectly, and branch regions can still bulge.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** Convolution is the first serious alternative to “one authored muscle equals one capsule.” A muscle group can be a curve or a thin polygonal sheet with a weight distribution, and its field can be integrated before the group is composed with neighboring tissues. That gives continuity under skeleton segmentation and lets the skeleton carry more semantic structure than a list of disconnected lofts. For deterministic use, integrate analytically for the supported finite skeleton family, seal the kernel and quadrature/error bound, and solve the iso-value or weight scale for a declared radius law. A branch whose prescribed cross-section cannot be met within the kernel's admissible parameter range must yield `IntegralRadiusUnsatisfied`, not a thicker threshold chosen by eye.

### 2.2 Bulge Elimination in Convolution Surfaces

**Citation.** Jules Bloomenthal. “Bulge Elimination in Convolution Surfaces.” *Computer Graphics Forum*, 16(1), 31–41, 1997. [Author manuscript](https://www.bloomenthal.com/JBloom/pdf/bulge-elim.pdf).

**What it actually does.** Bloomenthal analyzes why convolution and other implicit blend methods bulge at joins and branches. Convolution's linearity means that subdividing a skeleton does not introduce a seam: the convolution of the union equals the sum of the convolutions of the pieces. The paper studies conditions under which convolution surfaces are bulge-free, including the relation between skeleton-element size and the kernel, and provides techniques for modeling natural forms with crease-free joins while retaining smooth concave blending. It does not claim that every arbitrary skeleton and kernel pair is bulge-free.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The paper supplies a law and a preflight check rather than just another operator. For every integral surface, compute a finite ratio between local skeleton curvature/branch scale and kernel support; accept only if the ratio lies in a certified bulge-free region, or route the junction to a different local operator. This is exactly the kind of typed obstruction the current global blend lacks: a branch can be rejected with the offending junction and scale witness. The adoption is cheap if used as a validator around a convolution field; it becomes dangerous only when its sufficient conditions are silently treated as universal guarantees.

### 2.3 Creating and Rendering Convolution Surfaces

**Citation.** Jon Paul McCormack and Andrei V. Sherstyuk. “Creating and Rendering Convolution Surfaces.” *Computer Graphics Forum*, 17(2), 113–120, 1998. [DOI: 10.1111/1467-8659.00232](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8659.00232).

**What it actually does.** McCormack and Sherstyuk derive analytical convolution solutions for a larger family of skeletal primitives: points, line segments, polygons, arcs, and planes. They also outline ray tracing for combinations of these primitives. The contribution is practical: it replaces an expensive or undersampled numerical integral with closed forms for common skeleton elements and makes the representation useful for modeling and rendering. It does not solve tissue mechanics, preserve biological volume under activation, or decide which skeleton elements should belong to a muscle.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This paper reduces the implementation risk of a skeleton-integral surface. GOLEM already has finite authored spines and profile sections; a first convolution pass can support only points, segments, and a bounded junction polygon, with every other skeleton shape rejected at decode time. Analytic evaluation also improves deterministic receipts: no order-dependent quadrature accumulation and no sampling-dependent topology at ordinary resolutions. The derived field should carry its kernel family, support, and radius calibration; mesh extraction remains downstream. The right first cut is therefore a small closed integral vocabulary, not a general numerical integrator that can fail differently on every platform.

### 2.4 SCALe-invariant Integral Surfaces (SCALIS)

**Citation.** Cédric Zanni, Adrien Bernhardt, Maxime Quiblier, and Marie-Paule Cani. “SCALe-invariant Integral Surfaces.” *Computer Graphics Forum*, 32(6), 251–262, 2013. [DOI: 10.1111/cgf.12199](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.12199).

**What it actually does.** SCALIS is a skeleton-graph implicit representation designed to fix two weaknesses of ordinary convolution surfaces: they do not directly reproduce prescribed radii, and their blend behavior changes with model scale. SCALIS makes the radius around a skeleton explicitly controllable, makes blend regions self-similar under scale changes, and prevents thin components from being excessively smoothed when they meet large components. It preserves smooth integral-style blending over a graph while changing the normalization and local influence law.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the most promising direct literature match for a procedural creature kernel whose authored parameters are lengths, widths, and depths rather than arbitrary field amplitudes. A SCALIS-like field can turn a skeleton graph plus a radius function into a solved surface with a declared cross-section law, which is far more legible than tuning a global blend radius as a fraction of the bounding-box diagonal. It should own a separate integral-field representation, not be smuggled into `smin`; the existing engine can consume its field through the current pure sampling boundary. Deterministic adaptation requires a finite graph, analytic or reproducible integral evaluation, explicit scale normalization, and typed failures for radius conflicts, self-overlap, and unresolved branch topology. The likely payoff is high, but the implementation cost is above a composition-operator swap, so it ranks second rather than first.

### 2.5 Implicit Variable-Radius Arc Canal Surfaces for Solid Modeling

**Citation.** Oleg Fryazinov and Alexander Pasko. “Implicit Variable-Radius Arc Canal Surfaces for Solid Modeling.” *Computer-Aided Design and Applications*, 14(3), 251–258, 2017. [DOI: 10.1080/16864360.2016.1240446](https://doi.org/10.1080/16864360.2016.1240446).

**What it actually does.** A canal surface is the envelope of a one-parameter family of spheres around a spine. Fryazinov and Pasko derive exact and approximate implicit forms for an arc spine whose radius varies linearly with angle, and discuss when each form is appropriate for solid modeling. This is a sweep/envelope construction rather than a field sum: the surface is tied to a moving cross-section and its radius function. It handles a useful local family but does not by itself solve arbitrary branching, collision, tissue mechanics, or surface relaxation.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** Canal surfaces are a good local replacement for the current capsule/loft where the intended tissue is a single bounded sweep with a principled radius law. They can provide a radius-faithful base muscle or tendon segment without inviting global blending. Use them only on a branch-free, curvature-bounded interval; at a branch, hand off to an integral or bounded junction operator. The deterministic contract should include spine curvature, radius derivative, and envelope regularity checks. If adjacent cross-sections intersect or the envelope becomes singular, emit `CanalEnvelopeObstruction` with the interval and bound; do not let polygonization decide whether a self-intersecting sweep “looks acceptable.”

### 2.6 Analytic Curve Skeletons for 3D Surface Modeling and Processing

**Citation.** Jean-Marc Thiery, Bert Buchholz, Julien Tierny, and Tamy Boubekeur. “Analytic Curve Skeletons for 3D Surface Modeling and Processing.” *Computer Graphics Forum*, 31, 2223–2232, 2012. [DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.03215.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2012.03215.x).

**What it actually does.** The paper constructs an analytic curve skeleton from a disk-cylinder decomposition and a piecewise harmonic parameterization. The resulting skeleton has regularity inherited from the source surface and supports well-defined surface-to-skeleton and skeleton-to-surface projections. Demonstrations include local thickness editing, inset surfaces, shell mapping, and feature-preserving smoothing. It is primarily a reverse-analysis and processing method: it starts from a surface and derives a useful skeleton, rather than generating a creature from an authored skeleton.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The valuable idea is the projection contract. A solved surface should retain a stable, typed correspondence to its generating skeleton and local thickness coordinate, so later skin or detail layers can ask “which tissue interval owns this point?” without reverse-engineering a polygon mesh. GOLEM can use a simpler authored analogue: each integral or canal patch exports a skeleton-to-surface map and a bounded inverse map where regularity permits. This is a derived view, not a second geometry owner. Non-invertible regions, branch ambiguity, and projection multiplicity are explicit obstructions that downstream skin and vascular systems can consume.

## 3. Anatomy-based modeling lineage

### 3.1 Layered Construction for Deformable Animated Characters

**Citation.** John E. Chadwick, David R. Haumann, and Richard E. Parent. “Layered Construction for Deformable Animated Characters.” *Computer Graphics*, 23(3), 243–252, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH ’89, ACM, 1989.

**What it actually does.** Chadwick, Haumann, and Parent describe a layered character construction in which a skeleton drives underlying deformable components and an outer surface is produced from those layers. Free-form deformations are used to tailor muscle-like behavior, with layers separating rigid structure, deformable bulk, and the visible exterior. The contribution is architectural more than anatomical: it establishes that a character can be built inside-out and that each layer can have its own deformation model while remaining driven by the same articulated figure. It is not a modern volumetric solver and does not provide a universal implicit surface operator.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the original justification for refusing to make “flesh” one more entry in the positive union. A deterministic implementation can define a layer algebra—skeleton, contractile tissue, connective envelope, fat, skin—where each layer consumes the previous layer's solved section and exports a typed boundary. The layer is not necessarily a mesh; it may be an implicit field, a constrained volume, or a surface shell. The key law is ownership: a skin layer may wrap the flesh layer, but it must not rewrite muscle identity or skeleton placement. If a layer cannot be placed without violating its interface constraints, the failure belongs to that layer as a typed obstruction.

### 3.2 Anatomy-Based Modeling of the Human Musculature

**Citation.** Ferdi Scheepers, Richard E. Parent, Wayne E. Carlson, and Stephen F. May. “Anatomy-Based Modeling of the Human Musculature.” In *Proceedings of the 24th Annual Conference on Computer Graphics and Interactive Techniques (SIGGRAPH ’97)*, *Computer Graphics*, 163–172, ACM, 1997. [DOI: 10.1145/258734.258827](https://doi.org/10.1145/258734.258827).

**What it actually does.** Scheepers et al. model an articulated arm with a hierarchy of bones and muscles, and present fusiform, multi-belly, and pennate-style muscle models. Muscle bellies are based on ellipsoidal geometry, with dimensions adjusted as origins and insertions move; the fusiform model explicitly preserves belly volume while changing length and cross-section. The system encodes anatomical attachment and contraction intent in a compact procedural description, then generates muscle geometry for animation. The famous “volume preservation” is a modeling constraint for the muscle belly, not proof that the entire creature volume or skin layer is physically conserved.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This paper is directly compatible with authored intent because it separates origin/insertion, muscle family, and deformation law. The current `myology.py` already has the origin/insertion and section vocabulary; the missing piece is a solved muscle bulk whose cross-section responds to span and activation instead of merely interpolating a loft. Start with a closed family of volume-preserving solids and derive their parameters from the existing section records. Preserve the existing authored receipt as intent, add a solved receipt containing volume, length, and section residuals, and reject impossible bulk constraints rather than scaling one axis beyond its declared bounds. This is a cheap bridge from authored lofts to solver-assisted form, but it still needs a separate composition layer to avoid fusing every muscle into one blob.

### 3.3 Anatomically Based Modeling

**Citation.** Jane Wilhelms and Allen Van Gelder. “Anatomically Based Modeling.” In *Proceedings of the 24th Annual Conference on Computer Graphics and Interactive Techniques (SIGGRAPH ’97)*, *Computer Graphics*, 173–180, ACM, 1997.

**What it actually does.** Wilhelms and Van Gelder model bones, muscles, and generalized tissues as parameterized meshes or ellipsoids. Muscles are deformable discretized cylinders between fixed origins and insertions; skin is generated by voxelizing the underlying components, filtering the result, extracting an isosurface, and associating skin vertices with nearby components. During animation, the underlying components move first, the skin is mapped back, and an iterative relaxation process adjusts skin vertices toward elastic equilibrium. The method is hybrid: procedural anatomy, volumetric field extraction, nearest-component attachment, and a surface relaxation pass.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the clearest precedent for a deterministic inside-out pipeline with a solved outer layer. The voxelization step can become field sampling over the existing engine, while the relaxation step can become a bounded surface or level-set solve with a fixed iteration budget and convergence verdict. The nearest-component association should not be copied as an untyped tie-breaker: at an equal-distance or topologically ambiguous region, the kernel should preserve all candidate owners and report ambiguity to the skin layer. The method's non-uniform parameterization also suggests retaining authored local coordinates through meshing, so a derived skin surface does not erase the skeleton-to-tissue correspondence needed by later layers.

### 3.4 Anatomy Transfer

**Citation.** Dicko Ali-Hamadi, Tiantian Liu, Benjamin Gilles, Ladislav Kavan, François Faure, Olivier Palombi, and Marie-Paule Cani. “Anatomy Transfer.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 32(6), Article 188, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH Asia 2013. [DOI: 10.1145/2508363.2508415](https://doi.org/10.1145/2508363.2508415).

**What it actually does.** Anatomy Transfer starts from a reference internal anatomical model and a target character skin. It transfers bones while preserving structural constraints, erodes the target volume by a specified fat-thickness field, and maps internal bones, muscles, viscera, and fat into the resulting volume using harmonic/Laplacian deformation. The target fat distribution can come from MRI data or from an interactive editing tool. The method is semi-automatic and intentionally supports arbitrary target characters defined by a boundary representation.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The transferable idea is not the scan-driven source model or the editor; it is the constrained interior solve. A symbolic creature can provide a target envelope as a derived constraint and a finite anatomy template as authored or library data, then solve a harmonic deformation subject to bone, muscle attachment, and layer-order boundaries. This is useful for a later “anatomy transfer” feature, not the first surface-formation cut. The kernel must replace MRI-derived thickness with an explicit typed thickness field and reject a target whose boundary cannot contain the transferred anatomy. No transferred mesh should become authoritative over the skeleton or the authored tissue declarations; the result is a solved view with proof obligations.

### 3.5 Dynamic Implicit Muscles for Character Skinning

**Citation.** Vincent Roussellet, Nabil Abu Rumman, Florian Canezin, Nicolas Mellado, Ladislav Kavan, and Loïc Barthe. “Dynamic Implicit Muscles for Character Skinning.” *Computers & Graphics*, 77, 227–239, 2018. [DOI: 10.1016/j.cag.2018.10.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cag.2018.10.013).

**What it actually does.** Roussellet et al. combine implicit muscle primitives with physics-based simulation and Implicit Skinning. The muscle forms are designed to preserve volume while resolving muscle-muscle and muscle-bone collisions; skeletal motion produces dynamic effects such as flesh jiggling, and the resulting fields drive a plausible skin contact response. The paper occupies the middle ground between purely geometric skinning and full volumetric FEM: implicit surfaces provide the collision and contact representation, while a physical model supplies dynamic muscle behavior. It is still an animation method with parameter tuning, not a symbolic anatomy compiler.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the closest successor for a “muscle solver first, skin second” architecture. It suggests keeping each muscle as an identity-bearing field/volume, solving its span and activation constraints before composition, and using field contact rather than triangle collision as the interface. A deterministic static kernel can initially omit time integration and solve only rest or pose-conditioned equilibrium: preserve volume, satisfy attachments, enforce pairwise non-penetration, then derive a composite skin field. Dynamics can later be an effect interpreter over the same solved tissue state. The typed boundary is essential: collision infeasibility, volume residual, and skin-layer contact are distinct obstructions; a visually smooth union is not an acceptable fallback for any of them.

### 3.6 Computational Bodybuilding: Anatomically-based Modeling of Human Bodies

**Citation.** Shunsuke Saito, Zi-Ye Zhou, and Ladislav Kavan. “Computational Bodybuilding: Anatomically-based Modeling of Human Bodies.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 34(4), Proceedings of SIGGRAPH 2015, 2015. [Project page](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~ladislav/saito15computational/saito15computational.html).

**What it actually does.** Computational Bodybuilding lets a user change bone proportions, muscle hypertrophy/atrophy, and fat enlargement while deforming the entire volumetric body model rather than only the visible skin. It uses a quasi-static Projective Dynamics solver and a volumetric discretization designed to keep the degrees of freedom manageable. The output is intended to remain usable by downstream physics-based animation systems. It is a procedural, optimization-backed modeling system, but its human anatomy vocabulary and interactive controls are not themselves universal creature laws.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The deterministic idea is the choice to solve a volumetric deformation with a small set of semantic controls and to preserve interior validity, not the bodybuilding UI. GOLEM could expose finite controls such as muscle bulk, fat offset, and bone proportion as typed authored intents, compile them into a sparse energy with fixed variable order, and solve once per pose or rest state. Projective Dynamics is a plausible interpreter if its iteration count, convergence tolerance, and tie-breaking are sealed; it must not become an opaque numerical dependency whose last iterate is treated as accepted. This is a medium-cost route to global proportion changes after local muscle formation exists, not a reason to replace the current symbolic contract with a generic optimizer.

## 4. Production layered tissue systems: architecture lessons only

These are not substitutes for peer-reviewed algorithm papers. Production systems are valuable here because they reveal the ownership topology that survives real shots: skeleton drives anatomy; tissue layers are solved in order; cached results become inputs to the next layer; and a fast preview path is separated from the expensive final solve. They do **not** justify importing proprietary material models, opaque artist state, or a requirement for interactive sculpting into a deterministic kernel.

### 4.1 Weta Tissue

**Citation.** Weta Digital / Wētā FX. “Tissue: Physically-Based Character Simulation Framework.” Production-system overview and Scientific and Technical Academy Award record, 2013; current overview page maintained by Wētā FX. [Wētā FX Tissue overview](https://www.wetafx.co.nz/research-and-tech/technology/tissue).

**What it actually does.** Weta describes Tissue as a linear-elastic finite-element framework that builds a creature from the inside out: skeleton, muscles, fascia, fat, and skin. The system drives visible skin deformation from the motion and interaction of underlying structures and was used in feature production. The public description is architectural rather than a reproducible paper: it establishes the layer order, physically based solve, and production separation, but does not disclose a complete constitutive model, discretization, or acceptance algorithm.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The lesson is to make layer ownership explicit and cacheable. A deterministic GOLEM pipeline should derive `SolvedSkeleton`, `SolvedMuscleLayer`, `SolvedConnectiveLayer`, and `SolvedSkinLayer` in sequence, with each layer consuming an immutable prior result and emitting typed boundary evidence. A preview projection may use a cheap implicit approximation, but it must be labeled as a derived view and never silently promoted to final acceptance. Weta's FEM scale is not a cheap adoption; the cheap adoption is the same inside-out topology with a smaller local solver and a hard boundary between symbolic intent and numerical result.

### 4.2 Ziva Dynamics / Ziva VFX

**Citation.** Ziva Dynamics. *zBuilder Documentation 1.0.4* and Ziva Character Simulation workflow documentation, 2018-era software documentation, accessed 2026-07-22. [zBuilder documentation](https://ziva-vfx.readthedocs.io/en/latest/).

**What it actually does.** Ziva's public tooling exposes a scene graph of bones, tissue, tetrahedral elements, attachments, fibers, cloth, and solvers, and its documented character workflow proceeds from bones through muscles, fascia, fat, and skin. The layer outputs are commonly transferred as caches into the next pass; configuration includes material parameters, attachment maps, fibers, and solver settings. This is vendor documentation, not a peer-reviewed specification, so its exact numerical semantics and production defaults should not be inferred from the UI or from marketing descriptions.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The reusable architectural point is a typed layer registry with explicit attachments and cache boundaries. GOLEM can model each boundary as an immutable solved section keyed by skeleton/tissue identity, with attachments represented as typed relations rather than string node names. A future FEM interpreter may consume that section, but the pure kernel must still own the legal vocabulary, deterministic input ordering, and verdict projection. Do not copy Ziva's painted maps or scene serialization as a second authoring surface. If a tissue cache is stale, missing, or incompatible with its predecessor, return a cache/interface obstruction; do not reuse whatever geometry happens to be in memory.

### 4.3 Houdini Muscles & Tissue / Otis and FEM workflows

**Citation.** SideFX. *Houdini Muscles & Tissue Documentation*, Houdini 20.5/21 documentation, 2024–2025; specifically “Otis Muscle and Tissue Simulation” and “FEM Deform.” [Muscles and tissue documentation](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/muscles/index.html), [Otis recipe](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/muscles/muscle_otismuscletissuesim.html), and [FEM Deform](https://www.sidefx.com/docs/houdini/nodes/sop/femdeform.html).

**What it actually does.** SideFX documents a multilayer workflow in which modeled muscles are tetrahedralized, attached to bones, solved with Vellum- or FEM-like machinery, followed by fascia/tissue setup and skin post-processing with sliding. The `FEM Deform` node uses a quasistatic solve to reshape a tet muscle toward a target surface while avoiding inverted tetrahedra; the Otis recipe separates fascia and tissue resolutions and lets the tissue drive the skin output. The system is a production DCC workflow with high-level nodes, attributes, target shapes, caches, and solver settings, not a closed mathematical creature-form language.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** Houdini makes one distinction worth stealing: authored target shape and solved interior validity are separate. A GOLEM muscle may carry an authored target profile, while a volumetric or implicit solver derives a valid interior and reports whether it reached the target within tolerance. The resolution split between fascia and tissue also suggests separate proof cones: a low-resolution connective carrier need not be the final skin field, but its clearance and containment must be checked before skin formation. Do not import SOP networks or time-dependent attributes into the semantic core. The deterministic version is a pure problem builder plus an effect-boundary solver with fixed budgets and typed `Convergence`, `InvertedCell`, and `TargetResidual` outcomes.

## 5. Skin as its own solved layer

### 5.1 Implicit Skinning: Real-Time Skin Deformation with Contact Modeling

**Citation.** Rodolphe Vaillant, Loïc Barthe, Gaël Guennebaud, Marie-Paule Cani, Damien Rohmer, Brian Wyvill, Olivier Gourmel, and Mathias Paulin. “Implicit Skinning: Real-Time Skin Deformation with Contact Modeling.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 32(4), Article 125, 12 pages, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH 2013. [DOI: 10.1145/2461912.2461960](https://doi.org/10.1145/2461912.2461960).

**What it actually does.** Implicit Skinning applies a post-process to a conventional geometric skinning result. It builds scalar fields for body parts, projects skinned vertices onto the appropriate iso-surfaces, and uses the implicit representation to create contact effects, organic bulges, and joint corrections without explicit triangle-triangle collision handling. It is fast because it uses fields rather than a tetrahedral physical simulation, but the original method depends on an initial skinning solution and can fail under deep self-intersection or severe bending.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is a practical first skin layer after a solved muscle field exists. Instead of projecting an arbitrary mesh, define the skin as an iso-surface at a declared offset from the solved flesh envelope and solve each tracked point or level-set sample against that field. The current engine already has field sampling and iso-surface extraction, so the new authority should be a skin problem and verdict, not another mesh deformer. The key improvement over the paper's original setup is to remove dependence on an unverified initial skinning result: initialize from a skeleton-to-surface correspondence emitted by the formation solve, and reject points that cross the field's medial ambiguity or encounter a non-regular gradient.

### 5.2 Robust Iso-Surface Tracking for Interactive Character Skinning

**Citation.** Rodolphe Vaillant, Gaël Guennebaud, Loïc Barthe, Brian Wyvill, and Marie-Paule Cani. “Robust Iso-Surface Tracking for Interactive Character Skinning.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 33(6), Article 189, 11 pages, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH Asia 2014. [DOI: 10.1145/2661229.2661264](https://doi.org/10.1145/2661229.2661264).

**What it actually does.** This successor tracks the skin mesh directly from one implicit iso-surface to the next instead of reinitializing from a conventional skinning solution. It adds composition operators that model contact surfaces as part of the field and uses a tangential relaxation scheme derived from an as-rigid-as-possible energy to preserve distribution and elasticity. The method aims to capture sliding, return to rest shape, and extreme contacts such as bent fingers or colliding lips at interactive rates. It remains a mesh-tracking and animation system, with state across time and a production-oriented numerical pipeline.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The valuable decomposition is `field update -> normal projection -> tangential relaxation`, each with a separate law. For a static or pose-conditioned kernel, those stages can be a bounded descent over a skin surface parameterization: normal constraints enforce the chosen iso-value, tangential energy preserves authored detail coordinates, and a rest correspondence supplies the reference. Each stage can return a typed obstruction, and the final glue can prove that every accepted vertex or level-set patch remains on the target field. The stateful animation version should remain an effect interpreter; it must not contaminate pure body compilation with hidden frame history.

### 5.3 The Elastic Surface Layer Model for Animated Character Construction

**Citation.** Russell Turner and Daniel Thalmann. “The Elastic Surface Layer Model for Animated Character Construction.” In *Proceedings of Computer Graphics International ’93*, 399–412, Springer, 1993. [EPFL record](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/ffd7e689-25bb-4127-870b-d2ec8ad14f05).

**What it actually does.** Turner and Thalmann wrap a kinematic articulated figure with a simulated elastic skin surface. Reaction forces push the skin outside underlying layers, spring forces pull it toward those layers, and tangential freedom allows sliding over muscles. The same physical parameters can be reused across an animation, with a low-resolution interactive solve and a higher-resolution recalculation for final output. The model is explicitly a compromise between kinematic control and physically based surface behavior; it is not an implicit field method and not a closed-form solution.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** This is the clearest precedent for treating skin as a boundary-value problem rather than as the union of positive parts. A deterministic rest-pose solver can use the same energy shape: containment/non-penetration against flesh, attraction to a target offset, area or stretch regularization, and optional sliding constraints. The author should declare target offset and stiffness policy; the solver returns the surface plus residuals. Because a generic elastic solve is sensitive to mesh topology and iteration order, the first kernel adoption should use a fixed canonical discretization or a level-set relaxation with a sealed budget. Non-convergence is a first-class `SkinRelaxationObstruction`, not a final mesh with a warning hidden in logs.

### 5.4 Thin Skin Elastodynamics

**Citation.** Duo Li, Shinjiro Sueda, Debanga R. Neog, and Dinesh K. Pai. “Thin Skin Elastodynamics.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 32(4), Article 49, 9 pages, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH 2013. [Project page](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/research/thinskinelastodynamics/).

**What it actually does.** Li et al. model skin as a two-dimensional hyperelastic membrane on an arbitrary-topology triangular mesh. Their Eulerian surface representation avoids the need to constrain a thin skin volume to a changing body surface, while still allowing stretch and slide over muscles, bones, and tendons. The skin can be driven by an artist-prescribed or simulated body shape and added as a post-processing stage; low-resolution simulation can carry high-resolution detail maps. This is a dynamic membrane solver, not a base-surface generator.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The paper supports a cheap division of labor: form a stable solved flesh boundary first, then solve a thin skin shell on top of it. For static procedural creatures, use only the rest or quasistatic membrane energy and make dynamics optional. A typed skin problem should specify the body boundary, rest surface, material policy, attachment set, and resolution; its solution should retain the mapping needed for detail projection. The method is less attractive as the first adoption than implicit skinning because it needs a surface discretization and numerical solve, but it gives a principled route to sliding and wrinkles once the base field stops being capsule-fused.

### 5.5 Multi-weight Enveloping: Least-squares Approximation Techniques for Skin Animation

**Citation.** X. C. Wang and C. Phillips. “Multi-weight Enveloping: Least-squares Approximation Techniques for Skin Animation.” In *Proceedings of the 2002 ACM SIGGRAPH/Eurographics Symposium on Computer Animation (SCA 2002)*, 129–138, ACM, 2002. [DOI: 10.1145/545261.545283](https://doi.org/10.1145/545261.545283).

**What it actually does.** Multi-weight enveloping formulates skin deformation as a least-squares approximation over multiple bone influences, improving over simple linear blend skinning by solving for weights and transformations that better reproduce desired deformation behavior. It is a kinematic envelope method: the skin remains an explicit mesh, and the solver finds weights or coefficients rather than forming a new implicit tissue surface. Its value is controllable approximation and reduced artifacting, not anatomical tissue mechanics.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** Use this as a fallback or correspondence initializer, never as the authority for formed flesh. A least-squares solve can derive a deterministic skeleton-to-skin map from a solved base surface, with variables ordered by bone and constraints for symmetry, attachment, and locality. The result is a useful derived view for tracking or preview, but it must not override a skin-layer solve when contact or containment is active. Rank-deficient weights, negative influence where the vocabulary forbids it, or a point with multiple incompatible owners are typed mapping obstructions.

### 5.6 Shrink-wrap, envelope, and surface relaxation as baselines

**Citation.** Autodesk. “Shrinkwrap Deformer” and “shrinkWrap” *Maya Character Animation Documentation*, 2022. [Shrinkwrap deformer](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/Maya-CharacterAnimation/files/GUID-C2D83E32-2D69-478A-B159-6E1005480378.htm), [shrinkWrap relaxation](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2022/ENU/Maya-CharacterAnimation/files/GUID-2B3558DD-2EB2-4525-B716-885E859E27B8.htm). This is software documentation, not a peer-reviewed paper.

**What it actually does.** Shrink-wrap projects a wrapper object toward a target surface, optionally along normals and with repeated relaxation. Wrap deformers attach an explicit skin to deforming driver objects. These operations are ubiquitous because they are cheap and easy to compose, but they are projection and relaxation tools, not tissue formation: they do not decide the target's topology, preserve anatomy, prevent all self-intersection, or guarantee a physically meaningful thickness.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** A deterministic kernel can use the same operation only after the target field or solved flesh boundary is authoritative. “Shrink-wrap” should mean a typed projection problem with a declared side, offset, maximum displacement, and relaxation budget. It is a good cheap fallback for appendages and a useful finalization step for skin detail, but it is not a substitute for a muscle solver. Projection failures, foldovers, disconnected target components, and offset collisions must remain visible as obstructions. The architectural rule is severe and correct: never shrink-wrap an unsolved capsule union and call the result skin.

## 6. Skeleton-to-base-surface generation

### 6.1 B-Mesh: A Modeling System for Base Meshes of 3D Articulated Shapes

**Citation.** Zhongping Ji, Ligang Liu, and Yigang Wang. “B-Mesh: A Modeling System for Base Meshes of 3D Articulated Shapes.” *Computer Graphics Forum*, 29(7), 2169–2177, Pacific Graphics 2010. [DOI: 10.1111/j.1467-8659.2010.01805.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2010.01805.x).

**What it actually does.** B-Mesh takes a one-dimensional skeleton and key balls at skeletal nodes and automatically generates a quad-dominant base mesh. Subdivision and mesh evolution refine the result while maintaining edge flow along skeleton directions; editing the skeleton and key balls edits the base shape, and existing models can be composed by cut-and-paste. It is a mesh-generation and sculpting front end, not an implicit field solver, and it relies on user interaction for the skeleton and key-ball authoring.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** B-Mesh is relevant as a base-surface topology strategy, not as the semantic owner of flesh. A deterministic analogue could derive a coarse skin scaffold from a skeleton graph and radius witnesses, with topology selected from a finite branching vocabulary. The scaffold can seed an implicit or membrane skin solve, but the scaffold must remain a derived discretization of the solved boundary. Its key lesson is edge flow along skeleton directions, which helps preserve stable correspondences for later relaxation and detail. Arbitrary cut-and-paste should not enter the contract; composition should be graph gluing with explicit interface compatibility.

### 6.2 Sphere-Meshes: Shape Approximation using Spherical Quadric Error Metrics

**Citation.** Jean-Marc Thiery, Émilie Guy, and Tamy Boubekeur. “Sphere-Meshes: Shape Approximation using Spherical Quadric Error Metrics.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 32(6), Article 178, 12 pages, Proceedings of SIGGRAPH Asia 2013. [DOI: 10.1145/2508363.2508384](https://doi.org/10.1145/2508363.2508384).

**What it actually does.** Sphere-Meshes replace point interpolation in an aggressively simplified mesh with interpolation of spheres and their radii. The paper introduces a spherical quadric error metric in R4, whose minimizer fits tangent planes while distinguishing inside from outside, and uses a bottom-up approximation algorithm to construct a hierarchy. A sphere-mesh can serve as an interactive deformation control structure and as a compact volumetric approximation, but it starts from an existing polygon mesh and is not inherently a skeleton-to-anatomy formation solver.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** A sphere-mesh is attractive as a deterministic coarse carrier for collision, broad-phase queries, or skin initialization. Its inside/outside-aware metric can be repurposed for a skeleton-generated scaffold if the input tangent/normal evidence is derived from authored fields rather than a scan. The representation should remain a derived approximation with an error certificate; it must not replace the exact implicit field. A fixed sphere hierarchy could make bounded blend regions and skin projection cheap, while preserving a typed correspondence from each sphere to a skeleton interval. The adaptation is low-to-medium cost and useful infrastructure, but it will not cure fused capsules by itself.

### 6.3 ZSpheres and adaptive skin

**Citation.** Pixologic / Maxon. “ZSpheres.” *ZBrush User Guide*, software documentation, current documentation page accessed 2026-07-22. [ZSpheres documentation](https://help.maxon.net/zbr/en-us/Content/html/user-guide/3d-modeling/modeling-basics/creating-meshes/zspheres/zspheres.html).

**What it actually does.** ZSpheres provide an interactive hierarchical collection of spheres for quickly sketching an organic model. Once the sphere structure is formed, ZBrush can generate an adaptive skin mesh for further sculpting. The system is an authoring metaphor and a mesh extraction tool, not a paper and not a solver with a typed feasibility boundary. Its relevance is historical and practical: sphere-plus-bone hierarchies made skeleton-to-base-form authoring accessible before more formal sphere-mesh and implicit methods.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** Keep only the finite structural idea: a node radius and a segment radius can define an initial envelope around a skeleton. Replace adaptive skin's opaque mesh generation with a declared field or canal-surface constructor, then let the skin layer solve its own boundary. ZSpheres should not become a second public primitive vocabulary beside `gencyl`, `blob`, and `loft`; if adopted, they must lower into the existing canonical skeleton/radius representation or be rejected as duplicate surface authority. The result is a useful authoring intuition, not a new engine.

## 7. Recent procedural and neural work: only deterministic ideas survive

### 7.1 Interactive Modelling of Volumetric Musculoskeletal Anatomy

**Citation.** Rinat Abdrashitov, Seungbae Bang, David I. W. Levin, Karan Singh, and Alec Jacobson. “Interactive Modelling of Volumetric Musculoskeletal Anatomy.” *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 40(4), Article 122, 13 pages, 2021. [DOI: 10.1145/3450626.3459769](https://doi.org/10.1145/3450626.3459769).

**What it actually does.** Abdrashitov et al. replace individual muscle primitives with a volumetric segmentation in which every point belongs to muscle, fat, or bone. Muscle curves control the segmentation and automatically produce intersections and fiber fields; the volume can be converted to tetrahedral or surface geometry. The paper also includes an interactive skeleton-authoring tool that starts from a skin mesh and a library of bone parts. It is powerful but explicitly interactive and visually oriented, so the user-driven segmentation is outside this kernel's acceptance model.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** The deterministic idea is the tissue-label field, not the editor. A symbolic skeleton graph plus authored muscle curves or centerlines can define a finite multi-label volume, with a priority and interface law for every tissue pair. The field can then lower to the existing mesh path or to a volumetric solver, while preserving muscle identity and fiber direction as typed labels. This offers a path beyond a sum of positive fields: tissue occupancy is exclusive or interface-governed before extraction. The hard part is the label-interface solver; overlapping labels, disconnected regions, or a muscle with no valid attachment must be rejected explicitly. This is a promising later architecture, but its authoring surface needs a disciplined symbolic replacement before adoption.

### 7.2 Neural articulated-object implicit representations: a negative result for this kernel

**Citation.** Hao Zhang, Fang Li, Samyak Rawlekar, and Narendra Ahuja. “Learning Implicit Representation for Reconstructing Articulated Objects.” In *International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR 2024)*, 2024. [Proceedings abstract](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/e197fe307eb3467035f892dc100d570a-Abstract-Conference.html).

**What it actually does.** The method jointly estimates an explicit visible representation and an implicit articulated representation from motion cues. Its representation includes a skeleton, semi-rigid part assignment, rigidity coefficients, and time-varying transformations; regularization is used to infer the decomposition without a category-specific skeleton. This is a learned inverse problem over video and is aimed at reconstructing articulated objects, not at forming authored musculature or solving a deterministic tissue boundary.

**Adaptation to the kernel.** There is only one admissible lesson: keep skeleton, part assignment, and deformation parameters as separate typed factors rather than collapsing them into one latent shape. The learned inference itself is out of scope because it violates the no-data-driven and reproducibility requirements. A deterministic kernel can borrow the factorization as a schema for authored intent and solved evidence, with all assignments supplied by a closed vocabulary and all transformations derived by the existing kinematics owner. This entry is included to close the door properly: neural implicit geometry offers useful decompositions, but it does not solve the stated problem under the required laws.

## 8. Synthesis: three cheapest high-impact adoptions

The literature does not recommend “add FEM.” That is the most expensive possible answer to a problem whose first failure occurs at a pointwise composition operator. The practical order below minimizes semantic risk and implementation surface while moving the result from fused capsules toward authored-intent-to-solved-form.

### 1. Replace global smooth-min with a certified local composition family

**Cost:** lowest. **Impact:** immediate on every junction. **Adopt:** Gourmel's gradient-based operator, with Bernhardt's overlap-localized clean union/blend behavior and a bounded-blend support witness from Barthe/Pasko/Fryazinov.

The current engine already has the right effect boundary: primitive fields are evaluated, then `compose_union` glues them. Replace only the law, not the graph or meshing pipeline. Normalize the two input fields locally, detect whether their declared iso-surfaces overlap, and choose among clean union, bounded blend, or hard union using a closed operator policy. Keep the active region as derived evidence and verify that no outside sample changes beyond the certified support. This directly removes blending at a distance, reduces size-dependent bulges, and protects sharp features without requiring a new mesh representation or a physics runtime.

The necessary typed verdicts are small and honest: `FieldScaleUncalibrated`, `NoCompatibleOverlap`, `BlendSupportEscaped`, `GradientDegeneracy`, or `TopologyChanged`. A failed local composition must retain both tissue identities and the junction witness. It must never widen the global radius as a silent fallback. This adoption should be the first benchmark because it can be compared against the current `smin` on identical fields, with output parity outside active regions and allocation/time measured on the hosted path.

### 2. Add a radius-faithful skeleton-integral or canal formation pass for muscle groups

**Cost:** medium. **Impact:** changes the semantic primitive from “capsule with a profile” to “tissue generated by a skeleton and radius law.” **Adopt:** SCALIS ideas for scale-invariant, prescribed-radius fields; analytic convolution for segments and junction polygons; canal surfaces for branch-free variable-radius sweeps.

Do not begin with arbitrary numerical convolution. Define a small closed family: finite centerline segments, optional junction polygons, and variable-radius canal intervals. Use analytic formulas where available, and make the radius law explicit in the authored contract. A muscle declaration remains authored intent—origin, insertion, sections, activation or bulk policy—but the solver derives the field, volume, local frame, and contact envelope. `myology.py` continues to own declaration descent; a new solver should strengthen the existing muscle result rather than invent a parallel “surface corpus.” The engine consumes the derived field through the existing sampling seam.

The acceptance proof should include prescribed-radius residual, volume residual where requested, curvature/envelope bounds, branch compatibility, and field support. SCALIS is especially valuable because it addresses the exact scale pathology that makes global blend radii brittle. The likely first deliverable is a rest-pose muscle layer, not dynamic contraction. If a muscle family cannot satisfy its declared radius and volume constraints, return `MuscleFormationObstruction`; do not deform a neighboring muscle until the picture looks smooth.

### 3. Form a separate skin layer with implicit projection plus bounded relaxation

**Cost:** medium-high. **Impact:** gives the creature an outer surface that is not merely the positive union of internal parts. **Adopt:** Implicit Skinning for normal projection/contact, Robust Iso-Surface Tracking for incremental tracking and tangential relaxation, with Turner/Wilhelms-style elastic or relaxation terms as the static solve vocabulary.

The skin layer should consume a solved flesh boundary and produce an offset, relaxed shell. Begin statically: derive a canonical rest correspondence from the skeleton-integral field, project to a declared skin iso-value or offset, and solve a bounded tangential relaxation that preserves surface distribution and detail coordinates. Add pose-conditioned tracking only after the static layer has stable ownership and verdicts. Thin Skin Elastodynamics is a later effect interpreter, not a prerequisite for the first formed skin.

The layer boundary must expose containment, offset, stretch, foldover, projection multiplicity, and convergence. A shrink-wrap projection can be used for simple regions, but only against an authoritative solved target. The skin result is a derived view of flesh plus its own proof, not a new source of anatomy. If it cannot wrap without crossing a bone, leaving the flesh carrier, or losing correspondence at a contact, it returns a typed obstruction. This is the point where “looks like fused capsules” becomes a measurable failure of a named layer rather than a subjective complaint.

### What not to adopt first

Full Weta/Ziva/Houdini-style FEM layering, anatomy transfer from scan-derived templates, interactive ZSpheres/B-Mesh sculpt workflows, and neural implicit reconstruction all have useful ideas but violate the cost or authority boundary of the first cut. Borrow their layer topology, constrained-solve vocabulary, and cache discipline only where those can be expressed as pure problem construction plus an explicit solver result. Do not add a vendor scene graph, a learned latent field, or an interactive mesh editor beside the existing symbolic contract. That would create a second owner for surface intent—the oldest and least glamorous form of architectural rot.
